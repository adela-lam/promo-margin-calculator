How it works

Each unit you sell earns a contribution: the price, minus product cost, minus the cost of getting the order to the customer. An offer shrinks that contribution, so the promo needs more units, plus enough to cover promo spend, to earn what you'd have made anyway.

contribution per unit = price − product cost − cost per order
units needed = (units you'd sell anyway × regular contribution + promo spend) ÷ promo contribution
sales lift needed = units needed ÷ units you'd sell anyway − 1

Worked example

A $120 product costs $48 to make and $8 to ship, so it earns $64 per sale. At 20% off it sells for $96 and earns $40. You'd sell 1,000 units anyway (1,000 × $64 = $64,000 profit) and spend $2,000 on the promo.

units needed = (1,000 × $64 + $2,000) ÷ $40 = 1,650 units → a 65% sales lift
check: 1,650 × $40 − $2,000 = $64,000 ✓ (same as no promo)

Why a gift can beat a discount

A free gift worth $40 to the customer might cost you $12. That's $40 of perceived value for $12 of margin, while $40 off costs the full $40. Fewer, smarter offers protect margin.

Further reading

  • Marn & Rosiello, "Managing Price, Gaining Profit", Harvard Business Review (1992): across 2,463 companies, a 1% price improvement lifted operating profit by about 11%.
  • Blattberg, Briesch & Fox, "How Promotions Work", Marketing Science (1995): a review of what promotions do and don't do for sales.

Limits

This is a planning model. It doesn't count customers pulling purchases forward, shoppers who would have bought at full price anyway, or the long-term value of new customers a promo brings in. Treat it as the floor a promo has to clear.
